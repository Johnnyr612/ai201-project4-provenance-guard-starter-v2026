## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

1. A writer sends `text` and `creator_id` to `POST /submit`.
2. The route validates the request and runs the local GPT-2 predictability
   signal in `detector.py`.
3. `stylometry.py` measures sentence-length variation, vocabulary variety,
   and punctuation; its repeated-trigram signal separately looks for repeated
   three-word phrases.
4. `scoring.py::combine_signals` combines the three scores using the weights
   documented below.
5. `scoring.py::score_to_label` maps the combined score to a human, unsure, or
   AI message. The response includes both the component scores and combined
   score, plus a `content_id`.
6. `audit.py` appends the decision to `logs/audit.jsonl`; `/content/<id>` can
   report the current status, and a writer can appeal with `POST /appeal`.
7. If a creator completed the reviewer-approved verification step, the
   submission also displays their demo credential.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable my text is to the local GPT-2 model.
Lower perplexity maps to a higher 0–1 score, which means “more AI-like” to
this signal.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated writing
often uses likely next words and can be predictable to another language model.

**What it can't see:** Predictability does not establish authorship. Plain,
ordinary human writing can also be predictable, while unusual human writing
can score as less AI-like.

**Three checks before I wired it into `/submit`:**

| Input description | Perplexity | `model_signal` |
|---|---:|---:|
| AI-authored: “The implementation of the proposed framework requires careful consideration of several key factors. First, it is important to note that the system must handle a variety of inputs. Additionally, performance depends on several important variables.” | 21.6 | 0.7633 |
| My own question: “What does milestone 1 want me to do? Ok so walk me through how you would complete milestone1 and explain your answer for each.” | 73.2 | 0.3146 |
| AI-authored borderline candidate: “I think this plan could work, although I am not sure about the timeline. We should test a small version first and see what causes problems.” | 29.9 | 0.6577 |

The second input is text I wrote in this conversation. The first and third
were generated by the AI assistant for this check, so the third is not a
human-authored example. Its score is near the AI threshold of 0.65. These
scores are examples of what this signal returned, not proof of who wrote any
text.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** Sentence-length spread (sample standard deviation
divided by mean sentence length), type-token ratio (unique words divided by
total words), and punctuation marks per word. The style score normalizes these
to 0–1 and combines them: 45% low sentence variation, 45% low vocabulary
variety, and 10% low punctuation density. Higher means more AI-like.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** Generated writing can
have more even sentence lengths, repeat common vocabulary, and use
conventional punctuation. These are only rough tendencies, so I give
punctuation a smaller weight.

**What it can't see:** It cannot understand meaning or determine authorship.
Short texts and edits that change sentence breaks, word repetition, or
punctuation can move the score without changing who wrote the text. Type-token
ratio also tends to decrease as text gets longer.

### Signal three — repeated phrases (stretch)

**What it measures:** `stylometry.repetition_signal` measures what fraction
of three-word sequences repeat in a piece of text. More repeated phrases give
a higher score.

**Why it might differ:** Repetitive generated text can reuse the same short
phrases, so repetition can be a clue worth checking alongside the other
signals.

**What it can't see:** Repetition is not authorship. A human can intentionally
repeat a phrase, and AI text can avoid repetition. Short texts also provide
few or no three-word sequences to compare.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** Take a weighted average of the model, style, and repeated-phrase
scores. When the primary signals disagree, the average can move toward 0.5
rather than letting either decide alone. This represents uncertainty; it does
not make the disagreement disappear.

**The numbers:** `0.40 × model_score + 0.40 × style_score + 0.20 × repetition_score`.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my signals split, and what my rule does with it:** On the
generic AI explanatory sample below, the model score was `0.7633` and style
score was `0.3016`. The new repeated-phrase score and weighted average are
shown in the calibration table. The style and repetition signals reduce the
combined result compared with the model score alone. I prefer disagreement to
be visible, although this can let AI text through.

**Calibration checks:** I used the same five texts for all three signals. The
“borderline-style” and “lightly edited AI” rows are AI-generated candidates,
not human-authored borderline cases. The formal human-authored text is an
excerpt from the course brief; the conversational sample is my own prompt.

| Input | `model_score` | `style_score` | `repetition_score` | Combined |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Clearly AI: generic explanatory paragraph | 0.7633 | 0.3016 | 0.0000 | 0.4260 |
| Human-authored: my conversational Milestone 4 prompt | 0.3847 | 0.3908 | 0.0000 | 0.3102 |
| Borderline-style candidate: AI-generated | 0.6577 | 0.4503 | 0.0000 | 0.4432 |
| Formal human-authored: course-brief excerpt | 0.2460 | 0.1506 | 0.0000 | 0.1586 |
| Lightly edited AI candidate: AI-generated | 0.6074 | 0.4366 | 0.0000 | 0.4176 |

This is a small calibration set, not evidence that the scores identify
authorship reliably. None of these five texts had repeated three-word
sequences, so the third signal contributed zero in these examples.
`confidence` is `2 × abs(combined_score - 0.5)`: distance from the midpoint,
not a calibrated probability. The reader-facing labels use the configured
score bands below.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00–0.35, including 0.35 | We think this was probably written by a person. |
| unsure | Greater than 0.35 and less than 0.65 | We can't tell confidently who wrote this. If you wrote it yourself, you can appeal this result. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.65–1.00, including 0.65 | We think this was probably written by AI. |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
I don't want an uncertain score to sound like an accusation. The message says
plainly that the system cannot tell and tells a writer how to challenge a
result.

**Reachability checks:** I sent these three inputs through `/submit` and
confirmed that each label can be returned. These are demonstrations of label
coverage, not proof that the system can establish authorship.

| Input | Combined score | Returned label |
|---|---:|---|
| Formal human-authored course-brief excerpt | 0.1586 | `human` |
| Generic AI explanatory paragraph | 0.4260 | `unsure` |
| “The system works.” repeated ten times | 0.9357 | `ai` |

**Moment 1**

  approach to connecting one detector signal to a content ID and audit entry.
- *What came back:* Feedback on the route's responsibilities and suggestions
  for checking the detector score and audit log.
- *What I did:* I implemented the submission route in `app.py`, kept it limited
  to signal one for this milestone, and verified that its score matched a
  standalone detector run and that the decision appeared in `/log`.

**Moment 2**

- *What I asked for:* Brainstorm ways to measure writing style and combine it
  with the detector score for Milestone 4, then review my scoring design.
- *What came back:* Feedback on candidate text measures, score combination,
  and ways to calibrate the result.
- *What I did:* I implemented the style measures and two-signal scoring,
  chose equal weights so disagreement moved toward the midpoint, kept labels
  provisional for Milestone 5, and checked that `/submit` and `/log` returned
  and recorded both scores. For the stretch work, I set the final weights to
  0.40, 0.40, and 0.20 for the model, style, and repetition scores.

**Moment 3**

- *What I asked for:* Brainstorm clear, fair reader-facing labels and review my
  plan for recording an appeal without replacing the original decision.
- *What came back:* Feedback on label thresholds, request validation, and
  keeping appeal events separate from decisions.
- *What I did:* I implemented the label and appeal behavior, chose wording that
  states uncertainty without accusing the writer, and verified that all three
  label bands were reachable and an appeal left the original audit entry intact.

**Moment 4**

- *What I asked for:* Brainstorm implementation options for a repeated-phrase
  score, a content-status lookup, and a reviewer-approved creator credential.
- *What came back:* Feedback on the feature designs, test cases, and how to
  document their weights, requests, responses, and limitations.
- *What I did:* I declared the stretch items in this README before building
  them, implemented the features, chose a reviewer-configured code instead of
  a hard-coded credential, documented that it represents local demo approval
  only, and verified the behavior with focused tests and HTTP requests.

### Stretch items — declared before implementation

Before coding these extras, I wrote down that I planned to implement all three.
They are now implemented and tested:

1. **Third signal:** `stylometry.repetition_signal` computes the fraction of
   repeated three-word sequences. The combined score uses
   `0.40 × model + 0.40 × style + 0.20 × repetition`; the response and audit
   log include `repetition_score`. On “The system works.” repeated ten times,
   the observed scores were model `0.9929`, style `0.9000`, repetition
   `0.8929`, and combined `0.9357`.
2. **Content status endpoint:** `GET /content/<id>` returns the decision and
   latest status for an item, including `under_review` after an appeal.
3. **Verified-human path:** A reviewer supplies a code configured outside the
   repo as `AI201_HUMAN_VERIFICATION_CODE`. `POST /verify-human` records
   reviewer approval for a `creator_id`; subsequent submissions from that
   creator show `verified_human: true` and a `creator_credential`.

The code is a course/demo approval mechanism, not proof of identity or a
production-grade way to establish that someone is human. It is not stored in
the repository or audit log. Anyone with access to the code can claim the
demo credential, so a real service would need authenticated reviewer access.
The verification event is local to this append-only audit log; a production
system would store credentials in an authenticated account database.
